# Offer Monetization

An operating playbook for arranging offers into a sequence — what you offer, when, and how — so
that the cash from one customer funds getting and serving the next.

## When to use this

- Acquiring a customer costs more than they generate in the near term
- Payback takes months; growth is throttled by cash, not demand
- You sell only one thing, with nothing to offer a customer after the first purchase
- You want to add upsells, downsells, or recurring revenue
- Marketing spend has to come out of thin margins
- You want to raise revenue per customer and how fast they pay

## The core model

**The target.** Make more gross profit from a customer than it costs to **get and serve** them
**within about 30 days.** Better still: enough within 30 days to get and serve **two or more**
additional customers — at which point cash stops limiting growth. (Thirty days ≈ one cycle of
interest-free card float: acquire → collect → pay off → acquire again.)

**The trap it breaks.** In a normal business, the slow drip of profit from many customers
eventually pays for one new customer — so you can only afford scale if you already have scale.
Front-loading cash breaks that loop.

**The compounding.** Double revenue per customer × double customer count × double payment speed
→ roughly 8× faster growth. Triple each → ~27×.

## The four offer roles

| Role | Job |
|---|---|
| **Attraction offer** | turn strangers into customers (something free or discounted that converts advertising into cash) |
| **Upsell offer** | get customers to spend more, right now |
| **Downsell offer** | turn a "no" into a "yes" by changing price or terms |
| **Continuity offer** | keep customers paying on a recurring basis |

Any one works alone; sequenced, they compound.

## Build in stages, one at a time

1. **Get cash** — an attraction offer that reliably brings customers for less.
2. **Get more cash** — upsells and downsells that lift revenue per customer, faster.
3. **Get the most cash** — continuity that maximizes total spend per customer.

Sequence, not a finished machine: get customers reliably → make them pay for themselves
reliably → make them pay for *other* customers reliably → then maximize long-term value. A
"finished" model bolted onto a zero-cash startup collapses.
