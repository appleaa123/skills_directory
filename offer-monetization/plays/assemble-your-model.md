# Assemble Your Model

## When to use it
Putting the whole offer sequence together, and deciding what to build next. This is the
integration play.

## The method
Build the model **one stage at a time** — each stage must become reliable (financially *and*
operationally) before you build the next, because each stage's cash funds the next. No business
starts with a finished model; a "finished" model bolted onto a zero-cash startup collapses.

**The evolution:**
1. Get customers reliably →
2. Make them pay for themselves reliably →
3. Make them pay for *other* customers reliably →
4. Maximize each customer's long-term value →
5. Spend as much on acquisition as the numbers and your delivery capacity allow.

**The four build steps:**
- **Step 1 — Attraction offer.** Turn strangers into customers and cover costs. Pick from
  `results-based-refund-offer`, `prize-giveaway-offer`, `basic-vs-premium-offer`,
  `buy-more-get-free`, `pay-now-or-pay-later`. Then advertise it. Finding what works can take
  months.
- **Step 2 — Upsell offer.** Get 30-day gross profit well above acquisition + delivery cost.
  Solve the problem your attraction offer creates. Pick from `next-problem-upsell`,
  `guided-menu-upsell`, `price-anchored-stepdown`, `credit-forward-offer`. Offer it at the
  moment of greatest need.
- **Step 3 — Downsell offer.** Convert the no's. Keep the price and change *how they pay*
  (`payment-plan`, `conditional-free-trial`); or lower the price and change *what they get*
  (`feature-reduction-downsell`). Alternate between them in one conversation.
- **Step 4 — Continuity offer.** Get one more sale inside the 30-day window and stack recurring
  cash. Pick from `signup-bonus-subscription`, `free-time-subscription-discount`,
  `commitment-fee-waiver`. Best timing is sometimes *after* the first 30 days — that's fine.

**Warning:** when the model starts working, operations start breaking. Reliability means
financial *and* operational reliability — bring in someone who can build and run the delivery
team.

## Steps
1. Build and stabilize one attraction offer until it reliably brings customers below your cost
   ceiling.
2. Add one upsell that pushes 30-day gross profit clearly above acquisition + delivery cost.
3. Add downsells (payment terms, then reduced-scope) and start converting the no's.
4. Add a continuity offer and pull one more sale into the 30-day window.
5. Only advance a stage once the previous one is reliable — financially and operationally.
6. Once acquisition is self-funding, scale spend against delivery capacity, not a fixed budget.

## Example — a services business
- **Attraction:** free DIY plan vs. a paid done-with-you engagement.
- **Upsell:** an annual advanced-services tier, with a prepay discount and a community perk as a
  sign-up bonus.
- **Downsell:** a payment-plan ladder ($X down, rest weekly), and if still no, start free with a
  frontloaded subscription discount until the first offer is paid off.
- **Continuity:** a guided menu of service tiers with a feature-reduction downsell to a minimum
  package, so almost everyone stays for something.

Another pattern: "pay a reduced rate now and keep that rate for life" can act as an attraction,
an upsell, and a continuity offer at once — one clever combination doing several jobs.

## Pitfalls
- Skipping stages — each one funds the next.
- Building continuity before acquisition and upsells are reliable.
- Copying a sequence instead of matching offers to your customer's real problems.

## Metrics & targets
- Every stage: make it reliable before starting the next.
- The whole point: 30-day cash per customer that covers acquiring the next customer (or several).
