# Chapter 15: The 12-Tool Regulation Kit

**Domain**: ADHD emotional regulation — a matched set of skills for rejection-sensitive moments

*Content in this file draws on published clinical and educational material on ADHD, paraphrased in this skill's own words and tagged `[verified]` at claims traceable to that material. Untagged claims are this skill's own synthesis; `[practitioner-common]` marks community-sourced tools.*

## Core Idea
Twelve skills drawn from CBT, DBT, ACT, psychodynamic, and interpersonal therapy cover the full arc of an RSD episode `[verified]`. You don't run all twelve. You ask *"What am I feeling, and what do I need right now?"* and pick the one or two that fit. The aim is trust in your own ability to ride out a feeling, not emotional perfection.

---

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Selection Rule — Urgent vs. Lingering** `[verified]`:
  - *Formulation*: **Urgent, body-level** emotion (surge, panic, heat) → grounding tools first. **Lingering, heavy** emotion (overthinking, shame spirals, self-doubt) → reframing and relational tools. Urgent states need cutting through; lingering states need untangling.
  - *How to apply*: Name the type, then go to the matching group below. Operating version: [patterns.md Pattern 7](../patterns.md#pattern-7-the-emotion-type-tool-selector).

- **Group 1 — Grounding (for urgent surges)** `[verified]`:
  1. *Acceptance* (ACT): Notice the urge to fight the feeling, describe where it sits in the body, anchor with slow breath and feet on the floor, and tell yourself it's allowed to be here without being fixed. Many people swing between suppressing and spiraling; acceptance is the middle path.
  2. *Mindful Breathing* (ACT/DBT): **Inhale 4 counts, hold 2, exhale 6**; ground in the senses (name five things you see, four you hear, three you can touch, two you smell, one you taste); watch thoughts pass rather than chasing them. A few minutes of daily practice builds resilience over time.
  3. *Neutral Observation* (DBT observe skill, ACT defusion): Note the emotion plainly ("anxiety is here"), skip the verdict, get curious about where it is and what set it off. Prefixing a thought with *"I'm having the thought that…"* creates distance. Practice on low-stakes moments first.
  4. *Movement Reset* (behavioral activation): Shake out the hands, roll the shoulders, walk briskly — **a movement break of just two or three minutes is often enough to interrupt an overwhelm loop**. Keep a go-to playlist ready.
  5. *Delay* (DBT): Emotions don't require immediate action. Catch the wave early (tight chest, the pull to lash out or disappear), breathe 4-2-6 three to five times, ask for a short pause (*"I need a few minutes before I answer"*), and discharge the energy physically. Riding the urge without acting is also called **urge surfing**.

- **Group 2 — Reframing (for lingering spirals)** `[verified]`:
  6. *Thought Check* (CBT): Catch the harsh, absolute thought; name the distortion (all-or-nothing, mind reading, catastrophizing); test it — what's the evidence, and would you say this to a friend?
  7. *Resilient Reframe* (CBT): After disputing the thought, replace it with a growth-oriented story ("that was hard, and I handled it honestly"). Offer alternatives: maybe they forgot; maybe it has nothing to do with me.
  8. *Emotion Labeling* (DBT/CBT): Pause, pick a precise word (embarrassment, rejection, overwhelm, loneliness), say or write it, then validate it ("of course I feel this"). Imaging research shows labeling a feeling reduces amygdala reactivity.
  9. *Self-Compassion*: Acknowledge the pain, treat harsh self-talk as a habit rather than truth, speak to yourself as you would a friend, and use a physical gesture like a hand on the chest.
  10. *Trigger Mapping* (CBT + psychodynamic): After the wave passes, ask whether the sting came from the words, the tone, or an older memory they echoed. Log recurring triggers (being ignored, interrupted, criticized, excluded, slow replies) and plan for them — ground before known-hard conversations and leave recovery time.

- **Group 3 — Relational (for what happens between people)** `[verified]`:
  11. *I-Statements* (interpersonal therapy/DBT): Feeling → the behavior, described without blame → the need. Template: *"I feel ___ when ___, and it would help if ___."* Practice when calm; match tone and posture. Expect this to be among the toughest skills to use under pressure.
  12. *Boundaries*: A limit that protects your energy and sense of self — it governs **your** behavior, while a rule tries to control someone else's. Get clear on what you're tolerating, start small and specific, say it directly without over-explaining, expect pushback, and look after yourself afterward.

## Key Concepts

- **Challenge vs. Reframe** `[verified]`: Challenging removes what's untrue; reframing plants something more useful in its place. Use them in that order.
- **Naming vs. Judging** `[verified]`: Naming is a neutral label for clarity; judging assigns blame or worth. Mindfulness is the broad practice; non-judgmental observation is the attitude inside it.
- **Timed Delay Ladder**: About 90 seconds of deliberate slow breathing starts to settle the amygdala `[verified]`; a longer pause — minutes to an hour — suits cancellations and charged texts; the existing 24-hour reply rule ([ch08](ch08-emotions-rsd-relationships.md)) covers high-stakes messages. Match delay length to the stakes.
- **Boundaries Flex** `[verified]`: Overly rigid boundaries can turn into avoidance. In family-first cultures, a boundary may be a quiet internal shift rather than an announcement.

---

## Mental Models

- **Cut Through vs. Untangle**: A surge needs the body calmed; a spiral needs the story examined. Using a reframing tool mid-surge, or only breathing through a week-long shame loop, mismatches the tool to the state.
- **Rehearsal Beats Rescue**: These skills work under pressure only if practiced when calm.

---

## Anti-patterns

- **Running the whole kit at once**: *Why it fails*: Twelve tools at a flooded brain is one more overwhelm. Pick one.
- **Using boundaries as walls**: *Why it fails*: A boundary guides your own behavior; a wall shuts out connection and reinforces isolation.

## Worked Example (Study Depth)

### Case: The Cancelled Dinner

- **Trigger**: A friend cancels dinner two hours before, by text, with no reason.
- **Type**: Urgent (stomach drop, urge to reply "fine, whatever").
- **Grounding**: *Delay* — phone face-down for 20 minutes; 4-2-6 breathing; a glass of cold water.
- **Then lingering** (an hour later, "they never wanted to come"): *Thought Check* — evidence? They've rescheduled twice before, both times followed through. *Resilient Reframe* — "Plans change; the friendship hasn't."
- **Relational**: *I-Statement* next day if it recurs: "I feel thrown when plans change last-minute; a heads-up the day before would help."

---

## Key Takeaways

1. Pick tools by emotion type: grounding for urgent, reframing and relational for lingering.
2. 4-2-6 breathing and two to three minutes of movement are the fastest physical resets.
3. Challenge first, then reframe; name without judging.
4. Practice in calm moments so the tools are available in hard ones.
