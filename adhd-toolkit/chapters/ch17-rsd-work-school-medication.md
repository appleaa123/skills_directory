# Chapter 17: Rejection Sensitivity at Work and School — and the Medication Boundary

**Domain**: ADHD emotional regulation — feedback, performance pressure, and when treatment adds to skills

*Content in this file draws on published clinical and educational material on ADHD, paraphrased in this skill's own words and tagged `[verified]` at claims traceable to that material. Untagged claims are this skill's own synthesis; `[practitioner-common]` marks community-sourced tools.*

> **Not medical advice.** Medication is covered here by drug class only, to support a conversation
> with a prescriber. No dosing, no recommendation of a specific drug.

## Core Idea
Work and school deliver a steady stream of evaluation — ambiguous messages, feedback, grades, reviews — which makes them prime RSD territory. A short, rehearsed sequence (step away, ground, write the facts, delay action) handles most incidents. When skills alone aren't enough, medication can turn the volume down enough for the skills to work. A common clinical position is that it supports the skills rather than replacing them.

---

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Four-Step Work Incident Plan** `[verified]`:
  1. *Step away* from the screen.
  2. *Ground physically* — 4-2-6 breathing, feet on the floor.
  3. *Write the interpretation down* and separate it from the facts.
  4. *Delay action* until the intensity drops.

- **Three-Column Tone Check** `[verified]`:
  - *Formulation*: For an ambiguous message ("let's talk tomorrow"): column 1 — the literal words only; column 2 — the feelings they raised; column 3 — three explanations that don't involve rejection.
  - *When to use*: Short replies, unexplained meeting invites, terse feedback.

- **One-Minute Pre-Performance Routine** `[verified]`:
  - *How to apply*: Before an interview, presentation, or exam: three 4-2-6 breaths, a quick head-to-toe body scan, then anchor attention on one physical sensation.

- **Medication Classes for RSD** `[verified]`:
  - *Formulation*: No medication is designed specifically for RSD; clinicians treat overlapping features (ADHD, dysregulation, stress reactivity):
    - *Alpha-2 adrenergic agonists* (e.g. guanfacine, clonidine): a class that has shown promise; they reduce nervous-system overactivation, which can create space to pause before spiraling. Side effects include fatigue, dizziness, and low blood pressure.
    - *Stimulants*: treating ADHD with stimulants can indirectly lessen emotional reactions; in some people they raise anxiety or irritability.
    - *SSRIs/SNRIs*: not RSD-specific; can steady baseline mood and anxiety. Evidence for RSD relief is limited and largely anecdotal; they take weeks to work.
    - *Mood stabilizers and other neuromodulators*: when RSD sits within broader dysregulation; support is mostly clinical experience rather than trials.
    - *Hormone therapy*: can stabilize hormonal transitions ([ch14](ch14-hormonal-cycles-sensitivity.md)); risks need discussion with a clinician.
  - *When to consider* `[verified]`: reactions stay unmanageable despite consistent tool use; relationships or work are suffering; sleep, anxiety, depression, or body symptoms are affected; or shame and paralysis feel constant.
  - *Order of treatment* (a clinical position, not a trial finding): many clinicians start with behavioral tools and treat medication as a bridge that lowers intensity while skills are learned. The right order for any one person is a prescriber conversation.

## Key Concepts

- **Impostor Feelings** `[verified]`: In a systematic review, up to 82 percent of people reported feeling like an impostor at some point, with higher rates among women.
- **Perfectionism Withholds Reward**: One interpretation is that an impossible bar denies the brain any sense of completion. Deliberately producing a rough draft resets the bar.
- **Unfairness Stings Physically**: Unfair treatment can feel like a promised reward taken away — an interpretation rather than an established finding. Two to three minutes of movement helps discharge it before deciding what to do.
- **Feedback Is Not Identity** `[verified]`: "This is feedback on the work, not a verdict on me" — revisit feedback once calm.
- **Rest Without Guilt** `[verified]`: Rest triggers guilt when worth is tied to output; acceptance and grounding make rest possible.
- **Narrator Distance** `[verified]`: After a failure, describing yourself in the third person builds enough distance to see the event clearly.

## Mental Models

- **Silence Is Unwritten**: A delayed reply has no content until you add it. Write the facts first; the story second.
- **Skills Are the Engine, Medication the Dimmer**: Medication can lower intensity; it cannot drive the response.

---

## Anti-patterns

- **Stopping the skills once medication helps**: *Why it fails*: Medication eases intensity; when stress rises again, unpracticed skills aren't there to catch it.
- **Replying to feedback in the first hour**: *Why it fails*: The threat read is still in charge; defensive replies cost more than the feedback did.
- **Waiting for confidence before acting**: *Why it fails*: With impostor feelings, confidence follows evidence of action, not the other way around.

## Worked Example (Study Depth)

### Case: "Can you stop by my office?"

- **Trigger**: A professor emails one line asking a student to come by after class.
- **Four-Step Plan**: Close the laptop; three 4-2-6 breaths; write: *Fact* — one-line meeting request. *Story* — "I'm failing." Delay any reply except "Sure, see you then."
- **Three-Column Tone Check**: Non-rejection explanations — a research opportunity; a question about the last assignment; a scheduling note.
- **Outcome**: The professor wanted to recommend the student for a TA role. The evening wasn't lost to dread.

---

## Key Takeaways

1. Step away → ground → write facts → delay is the default work-incident sequence.
2. Generate three non-rejection explanations for any ambiguous message.
3. Impostor feelings are near-universal; act before confidence arrives.
4. Medication options exist by class (alpha-2 agonists are one class that has shown promise); medication supports skills rather than replacing them, and decisions belong with a prescriber.
