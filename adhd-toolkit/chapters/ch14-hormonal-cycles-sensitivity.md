# Chapter 14: Hormonal Cycles and Rejection Sensitivity

**Domain**: ADHD emotional regulation — hormonal influences on sensitivity across the cycle and the lifespan

*Content in this file draws on published clinical and educational material on ADHD, paraphrased in this skill's own words and tagged `[verified]` at claims traceable to that material. Untagged claims are this skill's own synthesis; `[practitioner-common]` marks community-sourced tools.*

> **Not medical advice.** Hormone therapy, supplements, and antidepressants mentioned here are
> decisions for a clinician. This chapter explains patterns and tracking; it gives no dosing.

## Core Idea
Hormones are slow, body-wide signals that set the gain on the neurotransmitters behind rejection sensitivity `[verified]`. When estrogen, progesterone, cortisol, or testosterone shift, the same text message can land as neutral one week and devastating the next. Tracking the pattern turns "what is wrong with me" into "my chemistry is lower this week" — and lets you schedule around it.

---

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Four Hormone Profiles** `[verified]`:
  - *Estrogen — mood and motivation support*: Boosts serotonin production and receptor sensitivity and enhances dopamine. When estrogen falls (premenstrually, postpartum, perimenopause), sensitivity to negative social cues rises, and imaging shows heightened amygdala activity in low-estrogen phases.
  - *Progesterone — the GABA lever*: Acts on calming GABA receptors. For many it soothes; for people sensitive to it, the premenstrual rise brings fatigue, low mood, and heightened rejection sensitivity. **PMDD** is linked to GABA-receptor hypersensitivity, with abnormal prefrontal and amygdala responses in high-progesterone phases.
  - *Cortisol — chronic stress load*: Persistently high cortisol impairs the prefrontal cortex, increases amygdala reactivity, lowers serotonin and dopamine, and with long exposure can shrink the hippocampus.
  - *Testosterone — an under-recognized stabilizer*: Dampens amygdala reactivity in both men and women; low levels (from stress, poor sleep, aging) leave threat detection more active.

- **The Cycle Map** `[verified]`:
  - *Formulation*: Four phases with different emotional weather:
    - *Menstrual (days 1–5)*: hormones lowest, estrogen beginning to rise — often relief.
    - *Follicular (days 6–13)*: estrogen rising — focus and steadiness.
    - *Ovulation (~day 14)*: estrogen peak — energy, or agitation in sensitive people.
    - *Luteal (~days 15–28, hardest in the final 7–10 days before bleeding)*: progesterone up, estrogen down — the most reactive stretch; research shows reduced prefrontal activity in this phase.
  - *When to use*: Planning workload, hard conversations, and recovery time.

- **Track → Pattern → Plan** `[verified]`:
  - *How to apply*:
    1. *Track daily* for at least one to two cycles — a cycle app, a 1–10 mood score plus triggers in a journal, or a color-coded spreadsheet by phase. Note conflicts, reactivity, and focus.
    2. *Mark bleeding start* at month end and look for recurring dips in the days before it.
    3. *Plan the hard window*: in the luteal window, reduce obligations, postpone emotionally loaded conversations, protect rest, and add small dopamine sources (music, a walk, creative play).
    4. *Use the high window*: schedule demanding meetings, goal-setting, and social plans when estrogen is higher.
    5. *Share the data*: a tracked record is especially useful to a prescriber if you take psychiatric medication.

## Key Concepts

- **Puberty**: The prefrontal cortex is still maturing while hormones surge — peak mismatch between alarm and brake `[verified]`.
- **Postpartum**: Within about 24 hours of childbirth, pregnancy hormones can fall by up to 90 percent `[verified]`, depleting dopamine and feeding the "baby blues"; low dopamine also plays a large role in postpartum depression `[verified]`.
- **Perimenopause and menopause**: Declining estrogen, progesterone, and testosterone remove buffering; estrogen withdrawal increases amygdala activity `[verified]`.
- **Lifestyle supports** `[verified]`: regular exercise, steady blood sugar (avoids cortisol spikes), tryptophan-containing foods (turkey, eggs, nuts), and daylight.
- **Clinical options for severe patterns** `[verified]`: hormone therapy, lab-confirmed nutrient repletion (e.g. magnesium, vitamin D), and serotonin-targeting antidepressants — all prescriber decisions. Over-the-counter supplements are loosely regulated.

## Mental Models

- **Same Message, Different Week**: Reactivity to an identical event varies with phase. Before concluding a relationship or job is failing, check the calendar.
- **Chemistry, Not Character**: "My estrogen just dropped" is a more accurate — and kinder — explanation than "I'm unstable."

---

## Anti-patterns

- **Making big decisions in the late luteal window**: *Why it fails*: The prefrontal brake is weakest and the threat read is strongest; conclusions drawn there often reverse a week later.
- **"It's just hormones" as dismissal**: From others or yourself. *Why it fails*: Naming the cause is meant to guide planning, not to invalidate the feeling.

## Worked Example (Study Depth)

### Case: The Monthly Performance-Review Panic

- **Pattern**: Every few weeks, a routine one-on-one feels like an ambush; the rest of the month, the same manager seems fine.
- **Tracking**: Two months of a 1–10 daily mood score shows the panic days cluster in the 7–10 days before bleeding.
- **Plan**: Move recurring one-on-ones to the follicular window; in the luteal window, block 15 minutes after any meeting for a walk, and use the 24-hour rule before replying to feedback.
- **Outcome**: The panic didn't vanish, but it stopped being a surprise — and stopped driving resignation drafts.

---

## Key Takeaways

1. Hormones set the volume on the dopamine, serotonin, GABA, and stress systems behind RSD.
2. The late luteal window is the most reactive stretch for many; PMDD is a distinct condition worth raising with a clinician.
3. Postpartum and perimenopause are high-risk transitions worth naming in advance.
4. Track first, then plan the calendar around the pattern; treatment decisions go to a clinician.
