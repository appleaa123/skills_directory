# Working With Agencies

## When to use it
When you have money and want to learn a new channel or platform fast. Not for renting a
capability forever.

## The method
The usual agency cycle — hire, get some results, results fade, cancel, repeat with a new agency —
loses. The version that works treats the agency as **paid training**: hire them to learn how
they do it, then bring it in-house.

- **No money?** Agencies are out; learn by trial and error (everyone starts there).
- **Some money?** Use agencies for exactly two things: learning a new method, and learning a new
  platform. They've already made the expensive mistakes, so you skip to the part that makes
  money.

**How to structure it:** start every engagement with a purpose and a deadline. Tell them
plainly: you want to do what they do in-house; you'll work with them for a set period (e.g. six
months) to learn it; you'll pay extra for them to explain *why* they make each decision; then
you'll train your team and move to a lower-cost advisory arrangement. Most agencies accept this;
if one won't, move on — but negotiate, because at some price it works for both sides.

## Steps
1. Confirm you have budget for a good agency.
2. Pick one whose specialty is the channel/platform you need to learn.
3. Open with the "learn it, then step down to advisory" proposal and a deadline.
4. Have your team shadow and document; internalize the capability.

## Example
To learn a video platform, a company hired two agencies at once: one to keep it accountable to
publishing while doing platform setup, and a second (at several times the price) to teach the
team the deeper craft. Both engagements were framed as time-boxed skill transfer.

## Pitfalls
- Believing "I'll never have to learn this because they do it" — that's how you stay dependent.
- Picking an agency that won't show its work or explain its decisions.

## Metrics & targets
- Success = your team can run the channel without the agency within the engagement window.
