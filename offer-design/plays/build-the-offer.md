# Build the Offer

## When to use it
Creating a new offer, or rebuilding one from scratch. This is the core construction play — do it
before pricing and enhancement.

## The method

### Think in options, not one answer
Offer creation rewards divergent thinking — many possible ways to solve one problem, one of them
much better than the rest — not the single-right-answer habit school trains. Deliberately
generate a long list of possibilities before you narrow.

### Five steps

**1. Name the desired outcome.** What the buyer actually wants (lose the weight), not what you
sell (a membership). Frame it as arriving at the destination, with a timeframe if you can.

**2. List every obstacle.** Walk the buyer's journey and write down every point of friction and
every limiting belief — before, during, and after using your product — in the order they hit
them. Then keep going: after they clear one obstacle, what's the next one? More obstacles = a
more valuable offer, because each becomes something you solve.

**3. Turn each obstacle into a solution.** Restate every obstacle as a "how to ___" statement.
"Buying healthy food is confusing" → "how to make healthy grocery shopping fast and foolproof."
The full list is your menu of possible deliverables.

**4. Choose delivery methods.** For each solution, brainstorm every way you could deliver it,
varying:
- level / group size: one-to-one → small group → one-to-many → self-serve
- how much you do: done-for-you → done-with-you → do-it-yourself
- access & support: email, chat, calls, in person; response speed
- format: live, recorded, written, tool/software, checklist, template
- speed and convenience

**5. Cut and bundle.**
- **Cut** the deliverables that cost you a lot for little perceived value, and one-off items
  that don't scale.
- **Bundle** the deliverables that are high value to the buyer and cheap for you to provide.
  Present them as one package, each item named and priced, the total value far above your price.

### Sales-vs-delivery balance
Doing more for the buyer makes the offer easy to sell and hard to deliver; doing less flips it.
Aim for the point where it sells well *and* delivers efficiently. On a first version,
deliberately over-deliver to make sales and learn — then systematize to protect margin without
changing the promise. Generate demand first, get people saying yes, and only then trim what you
offer or add friction.

## Steps
1. Write the one outcome the buyer actually wants, with a timeframe.
2. List every obstacle they hit before, during, and after — in order, in detail.
3. Restate each obstacle as a "how to ___" solution (1:1 with the list).
4. For each solution, brainstorm delivery methods (group size, DFY/DWY/DIY, access, format,
   speed).
5. Cut the high-cost / low-value deliverables and anything that doesn't scale.
6. Bundle the rest into one package — each item named and priced, total value far above your
   price.
7. Ship v1 over-delivered; systematize later without changing the promise.

## Example
A DTC skincare brand's obstacle list for "clear skin in 8 weeks": don't know which products,
inconsistent routine, no idea if it's working, give up too early. Solutions become: a matched
starter set, a simple AM/PM routine card, a progress-photo prompt at weeks 0/4/8, and a check-in
message at week 3. Cut: 1-on-1 dermatologist calls (expensive, doesn't scale). Bundle the rest,
each with a stated value, priced well below the sum.

## Pitfalls
- Converging too early — picking the first delivery idea instead of generating many.
- Building the offer out of your existing product instead of the buyer's obstacle list.
- Optimizing the offer before you have demand and feedback.

## Metrics & targets
- The buyer's reaction you're aiming for: "I get all this for only that?"
- Keep the obstacle→solution mapping 1:1 so nothing is unaddressed.
