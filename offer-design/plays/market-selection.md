# Market Selection

## When to use it
Before you build or rewrite an offer. Also when nothing you try seems to work — the problem may
be the market, not your execution.

## The method
The market you sell into outweighs both your offer and your sales skill. Rank of leverage:
**market demand > offer strength > persuasion skill.** A great result on a higher lever
overrides weaker levers below it; a genuinely bad market can't be rescued by anything downstream.

You are not trying to create demand — you are channeling demand that already exists. Score a
candidate market on four indicators:

1. **Acute pain** — they don't just want the outcome, they need it, and it's costing them now.
   The sharper the pain, the more you can charge.
2. **Ability to pay** — the buyers have, or can get, the money at the price you need.
3. **Easy to reach** — they cluster somewhere you can target: lists, groups, channels,
   associations, search terms. If finding them is like finding needles in a haystack, stop.
4. **Growing, or at least stable** — a shrinking market is a headwind that makes every other
   effort harder.

Three broad areas of demand always exist because the pain of lacking them never goes away:
**health, wealth, relationships.** Pick a specific, growing sub-group inside one of these that
also has budget and is easy to reach.

## Steps
1. List 3–5 candidate markets you could credibly serve.
2. Score each 1–3 on the four indicators. Drop anything scoring "bad" on pain, budget, or
   reachability, or that is clearly shrinking.
3. Among the survivors, pick the one with the sharpest pain and the clearest way to reach it.
4. Commit. Give it long enough to run real trial and error before you judge it.

## Example
A consultant can help "small businesses improve operations" or, more narrowly, "clinic owners
with 2–5 locations who just lost their office manager." The second group has acute pain (chaos
right now), budget (a running business), is reachable (industry associations, practice-management
groups), and is growing. Same skill set, far easier to sell into.

## Pitfalls
- **Market-hopping.** Trying one offer in one market, not getting rich, and concluding "bad
  market" — then switching. Most of the time the market was fine and the offer wasn't there yet.
  Switching restarts your learning from zero.
- **Being romantic about the audience.** Serve people who can pay you what you're worth, not the
  people you wish you could help.
- **Ignoring the trend line.** A market with three of four indicators but shrinking fast will
  fight you the whole way.

## Metrics & targets
- Narrowing your focus generally increases revenue until roughly the point where the niche's
  total addressable size becomes the ceiling.
- If you're below a few million in revenue, narrower almost always makes more money.
