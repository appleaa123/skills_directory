# Hiring & Onboarding

This reference is the framework an agent applies when
helping a founder make their first sales hire(s) and get them productive.

## 1. How to Know You're Ready to Hire

Readiness criteria: you don't add dedicated sales staff
until you've personally sold to a **statistically significant number of customers** yourself. That's
proof the motion is repeatable by someone other than "the product being amazing" — i.e., it's a
sellable process, not founder magic.

Once past that bar, the signal to hire the *next* role is a capacity constraint, not a calendar date:

- **First hire (market development):** your own calendar is not yet overloaded, but you personally
  don't have time to do enough top-of-funnel calling/prospecting alongside everything else you do as
  founder. You need someone loading your calendar with qualified appointments.
- **Second hire (account executive):** your lead-generation function is ramped enough that your
  individual calendar is *overloaded* — you don't have enough time left for down-funnel follow-up
  and closing conversations. That's the trigger to add an AE.
- **Later (account management):** you have enough existing customers approaching renewal that
  renewals/upsell start competing with new-business time.

Anti-pattern to flag for the founder: hiring interchangeable, moderate-bar, coin-operated
headcount, planning to fire the ones who don't work out. This is especially damaging
for an early-stage org selling a high-innovation product — the opportunity cost of a bad hire (the
demos a good hire would have set, the customers a good hire would have closed and who are now
locked in with a competitor) dwarfs the salary cost of firing them later.

## 2. Specialization of Sales Roles at This Stage

A hallmark of a modern sales org is specialization — it drives expertise and efficiency at each step.
Founders typically start out doing both jobs themselves:

- **Market development (SDR):** calling accounts, setting appointments with decision makers.
- **Account executive (AE):** sales presentation, demo, negotiation, closing.

Order of hiring generally follows: MDR/SDR first → AE once the SDR function overloads the founder's
own down-funnel time → account management once renewals become a meaningful time draw.

Note for the agent: more senior roles warrant a more involved hiring process; the kernel (profile →
sourcing → screening → interviewing → closing) stays the same regardless of seniority.

## 3. Articulating and Documenting the Hiring Profile

A **hiring profile** is not a job description. It's the set of raw and professional characteristics
that define the ideal hire. The underlying thesis: sales staffing should be held to the same quality bar as
engineering staffing, and hiring methodology should reflect that.

### Raw characteristics to define (used regardless of role seniority)
For each, look for concrete leading indicators in a resume/background — use these
when helping a founder write their profile:

| Trait | Leading indicators |
|---|---|
| Smarts | Referral by a trusted judge of talent; top/second-tier college; pattern of academic achievement |
| Resourcefulness | History of starting businesses, hacks/shortcuts, meaningful hobbies with a progression of excellence, paying own way through college, moving far for something new |
| Competitiveness | Prior competitive sports (esp. multi-sport, from an early age through college), high-end musical/academic competition |
| Coachability | Experience taking coaching — competitive athletics or similar |
| Likability/charisma/leadership | Leadership positions in teams/clubs/service orgs, prior experience winning elections |
| Detail orientation | Look for typos/grammar errors in the written screen and resume itself; ask how they organize their life (calendar/to-do list use, inbox zero habits, how messy their desk/room is) |
| Persistence | "Grinder" sports (crew, swimming, cycling, distance running, track), Eagle Scout, self-started business they stuck with |
| Positivity | Observable in how candidates talk about failures/difficult experiences during screening |
| Teamwork | Team sports, founding a business, service-organization participation |

"Grinder" persistence — the ability to grind through large amounts of unpleasant, repetitive work
(80 calls a day, back-to-back demos, clearing an email pipeline) because they know it matters — is
a distinct, essential trait alongside smarts and resourcefulness.

### Professional characteristics (for more senior/experienced hires)
Raw characteristics are usually sufficient for junior market-development hires straight out of
college. For more senior hires, weigh prior experience — but watch for these faulty-analogy traps:

- **Industry focus mismatch:** "sold into HR" doesn't mean transferable — match on *who they sold to*
  and *at what price point/budgetary tempo*, not the industry label. For example, a recruiting-tool
  startup should generally hire from recruiting-agency sales, not performance-management-software sales,
  even though both nominally "sell into HR."
- **Role execution focus mismatch:** "sales experience" isn't uniform — new-business acquisition (AE)
  is a different skill from renewals/upsell (account management) is different from full-cycle rep roles
  at less specialized orgs. Ask what fraction of the candidate's "sales experience" was actually the
  slice you need.
- **Sales cycle tempo mismatch:** a rep from a 15-day/"one call close" motion will struggle with a
  long, multi-stakeholder cycle, and vice versa — the muscle memory works against them.
- **Industry-bellwether mismatch:** be wary of reps out of the market's established incumbents — a
  seasoned enterprise rep from a household-name vendor often won't thrive selling an unproven startup
  product. They're used to brand-driven inbound and renewal-on-cruise-control, and the longer they've
  been there the more likely they've absorbed poor CRM hygiene. Exception: junior staff (e.g., SDRs)
  from these orgs can be well-trained and ready to move up, just underpromoted internally.
- **"Hiring a Rolodex" is a dying rationale** — with LinkedIn, Data.com, Hoovers/D&B, decision-maker
  discovery no longer requires an incumbent's relationships. Don't let relationship access excuse a
  candidate who fails other criteria.

Good non-obvious sources of professional fit:
- **Mid-stage startups in your space that hit escape velocity** — their staff proved they can sell a
  paradigm-shifting solution into an existing market once already.
- **Your own customer base** — people who lived the pain you solve (e.g., IT admins for an IT
  infrastructure product, recruiters for a recruiting product) paired with the raw characteristics above.
- **Achievement artifacts, not self-reported metrics:** ask for screenshots of CRM activity/leaderboard
  graphs directly from their org, not resume-stated quota attainment (candidates "sell you" on their
  own resume — verify with third-party artifacts).

### How to document it
Converge on the profile, then write it down somewhere shareable — a simple shared doc set to
"anyone with the link can view" is enough. It doesn't need to be fancier than that, but it
must exist as a shareable artifact (not just in your head), usable in job posts, with staffing
agencies, and for referral recruiting.

Illustrative reference patterns worth citing to a founder:
- **A recruiting-tool startup:** new grads from top/near-top universities with an achievement + athletic pattern, or
  SDRs poached from a bellwether competitor for a faster path to AE, or former technical recruiters
  (subject-matter expertise + high-activity habits). Deliberately avoided hiring AEs out of large
  established job-board companies — too used to presenting known, established solutions.
- **An IT infrastructure vendor:** pulled reps out of IT value-added reseller (VAR) shops — consultative, technology-agnostic
  hustle, low-margin inside-sales discipline.
- **Local-deals/marketplace companies:** optimized for volume/cost over technical acumen — junior grads from regional colleges
  with high graduate volume, for a high-activity, low-ACV transactional motion.

## 4. Sources of Hire

Different sources suit different growth stages. Use this to help the founder pick a channel mix.

| Source | When it's useful | Watch-outs |
|---|---|---|
| **Staffing agencies** (e.g., TheLions, Betts Recruiting, Rainmakers) | Best for the very first hires, when you have no referral network or in-house recruiting bench yet. Fee ~20–30% of first-year salary. | Recruiters are incentivized to place fast — proactively and repeatedly restate your profile, and push back hard on off-profile resumes or they'll keep sending weak candidates. Don't negotiate the fee down (it inverts their incentive to show you their best people). Limit to 1–2 agencies at once; use the "one in, one out" rule (they can't send a new resume until you've given thumbs up/down on the last). |
| **Referral recruiting** | Becomes your highest-volume, highest-quality source *once* you have staff and a network to draw from — not useful for hire #1. | Needs a shareable job posting staff can forward, a referral bonus ($2,500–$5,000 depending on seniority), and recurring reminders (bake into team meetings) plus proactive work (sit with staff and walk their LinkedIn/Facebook connections for profile matches). Close the loop: tell staff why a referral didn't work out. |
| **Job boards** | High-velocity candidate flow; even good passive-ish candidates browse boards. | Noisier signal-to-noise — screening rigor must be front-loaded. Tune the posting's stated screening rigor up/down based on whether you're getting too few or too many applicants. ~12 good resumes/week is a healthy rate. |
| **Direct sourcing** (LinkedIn, targeted talent-search tools, resume databases) | Best precision — you search for exactly your profile (title + company + keywords) instead of waiting for inbound. | Labor-intensive; usually not worth it until you have some recruiting bandwidth. Use combined title+company boolean queries, not generic "Sales, [city]" searches. Prefer email/phone over InMail. Candidates found this way are often passive — use a "sell, screen, sell" approach (excite them into agreeing to a process, then run the *same* rigorous screen as anyone else). |

## 5. Screening & Interview Process (Pre-Interview)

The goal: screening/interviewing exists to **authenticate** that a
candidate actually has the profile characteristics — not to build rapport or make a good impression.
Sequence, cheapest/most disqualifying first:

1. **Written screen (artifact-based, asynchronous):** ~12 open-ended questions, candidate spends
   under an hour. Not testing correctness — testing communication clarity, attention to detail
   (typos, grammar), and ability to structure a beginning/middle/end answer under time pressure.
   Sample question areas: something they've built that they're proud of, what team sports they played,
   an honest self-rating of how organized/messy they are, and an honest account of a deal that went
   badly. Delivered as a forkable shared document.
2. **Homework/voicemail pitch:** research the company, then leave a 30-second voicemail pitching the
   product as if the screener were a named prospect persona. Tests initiative, comprehension, and
   account-research instinct.
3. **Phone screen (~20–30 min):** for candidates who passed the written screen. Focus on authenticating
   intellectual acumen and resourcefulness by having them walk through a sales/lead-gen funnel
   they know (current job, or even a childhood business) rapid-fire: what were the inputs, who were the
   prospects, what's the competitive landscape, what would improve conversion, how would you solve X if
   you were "king/queen of the world." Devote the last ~10 minutes to selling them on the org if it's
   going well. Take green/yellow/red-flag notes immediately after — phone screens don't leave an
   artifact the way written screens do.
4. **Mock presentation/pitch screen (for closing roles moving from a presenting/demo background):**
   have the candidate run a full mock sales cycle on you as if you were a specific prospect — calendar
   invite, full 30–60 min demo, follow-up proposal. Evaluate discovery quality, tailoring, objection
   handling, whether they ask for the sale, and — critically — **coachability**: stop them mid-pitch,
   give a note, ask them to redo the section, and watch how they incorporate it.

Only after clearing all of these does a candidate reach in-person/team interviews — deliberately, to
protect the team's time from candidates who should have been screened out earlier.

## 6. Post-Interview Process

**Team interviews:** give each interviewer a specific lens (e.g., one rep interviews for
domain/subject-matter acumen, ops lead interviews for tooling aptitude, a socially attuned rep
interviews for culture fit). Script the questions so every candidate gets the same treatment. Every
interviewer records green/yellow/red flags plus a summary verdict (strong hire / hire / unsure / pass
/ strong pass).

**Social "beer" interview:** a final, time-efficient group touchpoint with the broader team beyond the
formal interviewers — surfaces cultural red flags, lets the candidate self-select out, and doubles as
early closing/relationship-building.

**Reference checks:** two types.
- *Candidate-provided references:* ask "On a scale of 1–10, how highly would you recommend this
  candidate?" then, after they give an 8–10, ask "What would they need to do to become a 10?" — that's
  where real signal comes out.
- *Back-channel references:* find a connection shared with you/your team (via LinkedIn/Facebook) who
  is closer to you than to the candidate — less filtered than a candidate-selected reference.

**Deciding between multiple candidates:** if more than one clears the bar, hire both if you have
lead flow to support it — in a greenfield market, uncalled accounts cost more than an extra rep's
salary.

**Speed after screening concludes:** once authenticated, move fast. High-quality
candidates won't stay on the market. If genuinely unsure about a candidate, the safest rule is: doubt
means pass — keep moving through the funnel rather than talk yourself into a maybe.

**Offer mechanics:** verbal offer by phone first ("people were positive on you, we'd like to proceed if
you'll accept") — get verbal agreement before sending the signable offer letter, so it isn't used as
leverage for a counteroffer elsewhere. State comp with its rationale rather than asking "what are you
looking for" first (keeps compensation principled and consistent across a hiring class, not
negotiated ad hoc).

## 7. Onboarding Structure

**Why it matters:** the traditional industry stat is losing 30–50% of a hiring class within six
months, much of it attributable to bad onboarding, not bad hiring. The opportunity cost of a rep
who isn't ramped is direct lost/delayed revenue, compounding as the market that customer would've
proliferated into recurs, refers, and grows.

**Format:** run onboarding as a "university" — a 1–2 week (or longer, for more complex products) boot
camp, run in **cohorts/classes**, not one at a time. Cohorts are roughly the same effort to run
whether it's 1 or 4 people, and they create camaraderie, healthy competition, and built-in sparring
partners for drilling. Give the cohort a shared identity — some companies have new-hire classes name
themselves after a shared theme, which reinforces the camaraderie. Curriculum starts as a forkable
shared document, highlighted section-by-section as covered; can graduate to spreadsheet-tracked
checklists or onboarding software as you scale.

### a. Pre-work (before day one)
Assign readings/recordings/homework to bridge the gap between offer acceptance and start date — this
both prepares them and keeps momentum against cold feet/counteroffers. Useful pre-work includes:
recorded libraries of strong and weak past sales calls, product demo recordings, and relevant industry
books on process, productivity, and performance. Budget
roughly ~10 hours over two weeks. Tell them explicitly it's tracked/audited, not optional. Separately,
there's pre-work for the founder too: have their desk, hardware, and welcome materials ready
before day one — first impressions set the tone.

### b. Onboarding 101 (day-one-through-week-one curriculum buckets)
1. **Standard admin** — W-2s, payroll, direct deposit, 83(b) forms; knock out side-by-side so it
   doesn't cast a pall over the rest of onboarding.
2. **Cultural onboarding** — an explicit, candid statement of what's valued and what's not okay, tied
   to the org's actual history (where the company started, where it is now, where it's going).
   Illustrative tenets a company might state: you don't have to be an engineer to operate with an
   engineering mindset; the sales team acts as the product managers of the sales organization;
   intellectual honesty is paramount. Ideally these tenets are *also* used as screening criteria
   during hiring, not just introduced after the offer.
3. **Business & market subject-matter onboarding** — three buckets:
   - *Market understanding:* the space's history, major vendors, adjacent/tangential solutions a
     prospect might ask about.
   - *Business driver understanding:* the client's cost/revenue levers and the metrics by which they're
     measured (e.g., for a recruiting product: cost per hire, time to hire, funnel drop-off).
   - *Technical understanding:* baseline vocabulary/technical literacy for the space (front-end vs.
     back-end, SQL vs. NoSQL, etc.) — enough to be "authoritative, not just a vendor" in conversation.
4. **Product & presentation onboarding** — in this order: informal product walkthrough tied to use
   cases and business drivers → formal sales-presentation training, chapter by chapter → live mock
   customer-facing demo, segment by segment, with each segment's business-pain purpose stated
   explicitly. Objection handling is deliberately *not* front-loaded as its own module — fold common
   objections into the presentation/demo sections instead, and keep a living reference doc for the rest.
   Competitive landscape review comes last, after the above foundation is in place.

### c. Tools & process onboarding
- **Provisioning (before day one):** all hardware/software ready and pre-configured where possible —
  desk, chair, laptop, monitor, headset, plus role tools (CRM login, dialer, email-tracking, presentation
  software). Keep a standing spreadsheet checklist. Add new hires to all recurring meetings in advance.
- **Configuration (group session, day one/two):** don't pre-configure everything — do account/tool
  setup (browser, email signature, plug-ins, voicemail, screen-recording tool, demo environment) as a
  live group "configuration party." It's more efficient in a room together and doubles as first-touch
  tool training.
- **Basic tools training:** browser hygiene (tab/window discipline), email (subject lines, templating
  culture, inbox-zero/archive discipline, keyboard shortcuts), calendar (compelling invites, calendar
  hygiene, "painting the calendar" — blocking the whole day deliberately rather than reacting to inbox).
- **Sales-specific tools training:** CRM data model (accounts/contacts/opportunities/activities) and
  the hard rule "if it's not in the CRM, it doesn't count" (doesn't count for credit, doesn't count for
  commission); key reports/dashboards; sales-enabled email (BCC-to-CRM, templating, open/click
  tracking); presentation software; power-dialing software.
- **Sales cycle & cadence:** explicitly walk new hires through how long a deal typically takes, who
  owns which stage (SDR vs. AE), one-call-close vs. multi-touch, and the org's actual meeting rhythm
  (standups, weekly team meeting, pipeline meeting, all-hands) — new hires shouldn't have to infer the
  process by osmosis.

### d. Drilling, repetitions, and shadowing
Presenting slides once, even with a quiz, is not sufficient — muscle memory requires repetition.
Three escalating formats:
1. **Group drilling:** each rep presents each chapter of the presentation/demo to the full cohort,
   stopping for instructor + peer feedback after each section. Slow, done once, very high-signal.
2. **Sparring:** pair up, trade presenter/prospect roles, run it in "game situation" mode using the
   actual tools (phone, presentation software) rather than talking across a table. Playing the
   "prospect" is itself valuable training — it builds the instinct to think like the buyer.
3. **Pair programming / ride-alongs:** new hire spends a day seated with a seasoned rep in the same
   role, watching live workflow (prospecting, task execution, calls) in a production environment — not
   a substitute for drilling, but a complement to it, done after the formal drilling stages.

### e. Going live: bluebirding, ramp monitoring
- **Bluebirds:** assign new AEs high-probability inbound opportunities first, with a senior rep
  teaming as backup (not running the call) — presence of backup alone often makes intervention
  unnecessary. Keep a printed script/outline on the desk as a recovery aid, not a script to read
  verbatim.
- **Throwaways:** unqualified inbound leads make useful low-stakes practice reps — but coach reps to
  run them as full real calls (discovery through to disqualification), not skip steps because it "doesn't
  count."
- **Call review without live teaming:** record calls (using call-recording/coaching tools like
  ClearSlide, Chorus, or Gong); institutionalize reps self-flagging their best/worst calls in standup
  rather than the manager auditing everything; sit physically near the team or roam with a laptop for
  ambient oversight instead of constant shadowing (full-time teaming is an expensive habit that often
  substitutes for real management work).
- **KPI tracking + regular 1:1s:** as reps ramp, shift from high-touch call monitoring to the standard
  KPI set (hold rates, win/loss ratio, activity levels, stuck-opportunity counts) reviewed in cadenced,
  ideally biweekly, one-on-ones.

### f. Ongoing learning & development (post-onboarding)
Onboarding is the most intensive learning period, but it isn't the end of investment.
- **A standing calendar block** (e.g., end-of-day Friday) for continued drilling — SDRs
  practice objection handling/messaging/demo skills for future AE-readiness; AEs drill
  discovery/negotiation/presentation with managers and peers.
- **Product releases and market changes get the same explicit-reprocessing treatment as onboarding
  content** — a short slot in the recurring team meeting for what shipped, plus dedicated sessions for
  big releases or major competitive moves, so reps aren't "frozen in amber" at their onboarding date.
- **SDR-to-AE upwelling** is a deliberate strategic asset: training junior reps into closing roles over
  6–12 months builds culture-native talent and reduces reliance on expensive external AE hiring.

## 8. Elicitation Questions for the Agent to Ask the Founder

Use these to gather what's needed to produce a real hiring profile and onboarding plan rather than a
generic template — pull directly from what the founder already knows about their own sales motion:

**Readiness / role**
- Have you personally closed a statistically meaningful number of customers using a repeatable process?
- What sales activities are currently consuming your time that a hire should take over first —
  top-of-funnel prospecting/appointment-setting, or down-funnel demo/close work?
- Is your calendar currently overloaded on outbound, or on down-funnel follow-up and closing?

**Profile**
- Who, specifically, sells something structurally similar to what you sell — same buyer, similar price
  point, similar budget cycle — that you could source candidates from?
- What does a "bad hire" look like for your specific sale — long cycle vs. transactional, technical vs.
  non-technical buyer?
- Is there a customer segment (your own users) who live the pain you solve and might make strong
  hires?
- What raw traits (grinder quotient, resourcefulness, coachability, etc.) matter most for your specific
  sales motion, and do you have any early signal on what "good" looks like from selling it yourself?

**Sourcing**
- Do you have an existing network or staff to draw referrals from, or is this your very first hire?
- What's your budget tolerance for a staffing-agency fee (20–30% of first-year salary) versus the time
  cost of direct sourcing or job-board screening?

**Process**
- What does your current sales process look like — cycle length, number of touches, who talks to whom
  — that a new hire needs to learn?
- What tools are already in place (CRM, dialer, presentation software, email tracking) that onboarding
  needs to cover?
- Do you have any existing sales artifacts (recorded demo calls, written pitch decks, competitive
  battlecards) that could become pre-work or onboarding material, or do those need to be built first?
- Are you hiring one person or a class/cohort? If solo, how will you replicate the sparring/shadowing
  pieces of onboarding that normally rely on a cohort?

**Onboarding logistics**
- What's realistic for your stage: a full 1–2 week boot camp, or a compressed version — and what would
  you cut first if you had to compress it?
- Who besides you (if anyone) can run pieces of onboarding (product walkthrough, tool training, culture)?
