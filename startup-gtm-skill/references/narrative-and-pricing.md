# Narrative & Pricing Methodology

Operating framework for helping a founder build their sales narrative and set initial pricing. Follow the sequence — each step builds on the one before it (an inductive approach: if a prospect disagrees at step 1, stop there rather than rehearsing the rest of the pitch).

## 0. Framing

The narrative is not fundraising copy or a mission statement — it's a specific problem → solution → specifics argument that will get recast into slides, email templates, spoken pitch, website copy, and video. It is a hypothesis, not a finished artifact: expect it to change as it meets the market. Still produce a coherent rough draft now.

Two illustrative one-liners to calibrate tone (crisp, problem-first, no jargon):
- A local-deals startup: small businesses struggle to bring in new customers cost-effectively, so the company aggregates demand across a large email list and drives it to merchants via a daily coupon offer.
- A CRM vendor: sales reps juggling many concurrent deals drop balls and lose revenue, so the company built software that keeps those conversations organized and helps reps book more of what they're working.

## 1. The Narrative-Building Sequence

Walk the founder through these seven components, in this order. Do not skip ahead to "how it works" before the problem/cost/status-quo groundwork is laid — the proof section depends on the cost section using the same units.

### 1.1 What is the problem?
State the business pain as crisply as possible so the audience can self-select in or out fast. Test: pose it to someone in the industry — "Have you encountered this?" — and they should say yes and keep talking, unprompted.

Elicitation questions:
- "In one or two sentences, what's the pain — no jargon, no product mention?"
- "If you said that to a stranger in the industry, would they say 'yes, absolutely' and keep talking?"
- "Are there nested/advanced levels of this problem for more sophisticated buyers?" (e.g., a passive-candidate recruiting tool's baseline pain — finding, contacting, and tracking engineering talent — has deeper versions for more advanced recruiting orgs.)

### 1.2 Who has the problem?
Identify the specific title/role with *functional responsibility* for solving it — not everyone who is merely affected by it. Rule of thumb: the person who controls budget for this pain, or who personally burns meaningful labor hours on it. As companies scale, the owner of the pain specializes (e.g., a founder-CEO doing sales → a Director of Sales → a dedicated Sales Ops role).

Elicitation questions:
- "Whose job title, specifically, is judged on whether this problem gets solved?"
- "If they told you solving it wasn't really their job, would they be right?"
- "Does the owner of this pain change as the company grows (e.g., founder → manager → dedicated function)?"
- Flag for later qualification: having *someone* with the title doesn't mean the org is big enough to be worth the sales motion (e.g., a solo office-manager/support-rep is not equivalent to a 12-person support team).

### 1.3 What does the problem cost?
Quantify cost of the problem, or cost of *not* solving it — this becomes the ROI argument. Push for a real number, not a vibe. Three tiers, in order of how easy they are to sell on:
- **Hard ROI** — direct, obvious cost reduction (e.g., datacenter storage cost per employee added; cost of support headcount per ticket volume; cost of sales reps needed per dollar of revenue).
- **Soft ROI / opportunity cost** — value lost by not acting (e.g., churned customers from under-support; incremental deals a rep could close with more efficiency — for example, reps going from 8 deals/mo at $8,000 avg deal to 10 deals/mo is a 25% lift, or $16,000/rep/mo incremental).
- **Directional/hard-to-quantify** — "increased agility"-style claims; acknowledge these are the weakest form of proof and should not anchor the pitch alone.

Elicitation questions:
- "What's the unit cost of this problem — per employee, per deal, per ticket, per hire, per month?"
- "Is this a hard cost (money literally spent) or an opportunity cost (money/deals/customers lost)?"
- "Can this unit cost be scaled up/down by company size to size an individual deal's value?"
- At minimum: get the *unit cost* even if you can't get the aggregate — that's the throughline for positioning value later.

### 1.4 How is this solved today, and why does that fail?
Identify where the prospect sits on this maturity ladder, and articulate the specific shortfall of each stage — with genuine credit for what that path does well (credibility requires acknowledging real strengths, e.g., agencies are great for immediate candidate flow).

1. **No solution** — the prospect doesn't solve this problem at all. Sales job = prove the non-solution is costly (tie back to 1.3).
2. **Solved via process** — manual workarounds using generic tools (e.g., a recruiting-tool prospect's staff manually searching GitHub, Stack Overflow, and Twitter for candidates, then emailing by hand). Failure mode: time cost + fragility (self-reported, no audit trail, missed candidates).
3. **Solved via service provider** — outsourced to an agency/firm that takes a margin (e.g., recruiting agencies commonly charge roughly 25% of first-year salary — a $37,500 fee on a $150k hire). Failure mode: cost, since the provider must earn margin.
4. **Solved via product** — competitors or adjacent tools already in place (e.g., a recruiting tool facing job boards, resume databases, and LinkedIn). This is actually a *good* qualification signal — budget already exists for this problem class. Requires knowing your competitive set well enough to not be surprised, without over-investing in exhaustive competitive intel (diminishing returns).

Elicitation questions:
- "Today, absent your product, how does the prospect actually solve this — nothing, manual process, outsourced service, or a competing product?"
- "What specifically breaks down in their current approach — time cost, dollar cost, error rate, coverage gaps?"
- "What does their current approach get right that you should acknowledge rather than dismiss?"

### 1.5 What has changed that makes a new solution possible/necessary now?
Identify the underlying shift — technological, behavioral, or economic — that makes your solution newly viable or newly required. This is what makes the pitch a *now* story instead of a "why hasn't this existed" story, and it points to market trends worth tracking (do they strengthen or undercut your narrative over time?).

Illustrative examples of this kind of shift:
- A CRM vendor: ubiquitous web/browser access enabled a SaaS CRM instead of clunky on-prem software.
- A recruiting tool: the rise of implicit professional activity online (Q&A forums, professional social posts, code-hosting profiles) — driven by growth of digitized professional output — made passive candidates newly discoverable.
- A storage vendor: falling price of flash memory made all-flash datacenter appliances cost-effective.
- E-commerce/mobile: the shift to smartphone browsing didn't just enable new solutions, it *demanded* them (pressure on e-commerce brands to go mobile-first).

Elicitation questions:
- "What changed in technology, cost curves, or user behavior in roughly the last 5-10 years that makes this possible or urgent now, that wasn't true before?"
- "Is this change still accelerating (strengthening your narrative over time) or is it a one-time unlock?"
- "Does this change enable your solution, or does it create outright demand/pressure for one?"

### 1.6 How does the new solution work?
Founders usually know this cold — the harder task is explaining it simply. Best technique: analogize to a solution the prospect already understands, then state the delta.

Illustrative examples of this technique:
- A CRM vendor anchoring to the category prospects already know: it works like a traditional CRM, but browser-based, accessible anywhere, always up to date, and far less clunky.
- A local-deals startup anchoring to direct marketing: it has built email lists of tens of thousands of would-be customers in the merchant's geography, reached with a daily coupon deal that drives them in the door.

Elicitation questions:
- "What's the closest thing the prospect already understands that you can anchor the explanation to?"
- "In one sentence, what's the delta between your approach and that reference point?"

### 1.7 Qualitative & quantitative proof it's better
Return to the exact cost/metric language established in step 1.3 and show your solution wins on those same terms — it does more of X or requires less of Y, in units the buyer already tracks. Prioritize metrics that matter to the actual buyer over vanity metrics (a headline count like "200 million profiles" is meaningless if only a dozen are relevant candidates for the buyer's niche).

Illustrative proof stack for a recruiting tool:
- Search recall: 5x more results than the market-leading incumbent for a query like "Ruby on Rails, SF Bay Area."
- Contactability: 60% of profiles include a personal email (vs. capped in-platform messaging).
- Outreach efficacy: 3x-5x better open/click/response rates via personal email vs. generic in-platform messages.
- Volume: unlimited outreach vs. an incumbent's monthly messaging cap.
- Social proof: hundreds of customers, thousands of hires, press/analyst accolades.

Illustrative proof stack for a CRM vendor: 3x-10x more frequent logins/updates than legacy systems; 20%-50% higher rep win rates; 30%-50% reduction in missed forecasts for managers; consistent analyst-report leadership.

Proof hierarchy to apply:
1. Quantitative, apples-to-apples metric comparison (strongest — reuse the buyer's own units).
2. Qualitative differentiation, ideally still backed by a metric (e.g., "better usability" backed by logins/day).
3. Third-party validation as a supplement, not the core: customer counts, testimonials (ideally citing the same metrics/qualitative claims), case studies, press/analyst coverage.

Elicitation questions:
- "Using the exact same units from the cost section (step 1.3), how does your solution outperform the current approach — a specific multiple, percentage, or dollar figure?"
- "What metric would the buyer instinctively reach for to judge you, and do you have a number for it?"
- "What qualitative claims are you making, and can you attach a metric to each one (usage frequency, data quality, etc.) rather than leaving them as unsupported assertions?"
- "What third-party proof exists (customer count, testimonial, press, analyst mention) that echoes these same claims?"

### Target shape for the output
Aim for a full narrative walkthrough across all seven steps that stays roughly one to three sentences per section, plugging in one hard number wherever possible — that density is the target shape/length for the one-pager output.

## 2. Pricing Framework

### 2.1 Charge from day one
Do not go free/unpriced, even at the earliest stage. Rationale: if it's free, prospects won't take it seriously, won't think hard about the value, and won't use it — there's no cost to them for ignoring it. Freemium tiers, lighthouse-customer discounts, or paid pilots with structured feedback are all fine; going unpriced entirely is not. Founders avoid pricing conversations because they don't want to hear "no" — that avoidance is itself the problem to push past.

### 2.2 Start underpriced and iterate up — don't price to perfection
Bias toward giving away more value than you capture, initially. Goal: get customers in, using the product, validating the value promise. Each sales conversation is a market-reaction data point — if a price lands with no pushback, raise it next time.

Illustrative pricing progression for an early-stage recruiting tool: **$99/mo → $199/mo → $299/mo → $399/mo → $499/mo**, ultimately settling near **$6,000/seat/year** on annual contracts paid up front (vs. roughly $8,000/year for a market-leading incumbent — positioned as beating the incumbent's capability while costing 25% less). Price moved up in step with the product improving substantially between rounds.

When you raise prices, grandfather existing customers at their original rate — don't punish early believers, and use the impending increase as urgency ("it's $500/mo now, but that may go up as we add functionality — buy now and you're grandfathered in").

**Signal to know you're pricing too low**: prospects say "oh, that's easy, sure" with zero hesitation — you're leaving money on the table.
**Signal to know you're pricing too high ("pricing to perfection")**: prospects nod through the whole pitch then visibly blanch at the price; win rates suffer; renewal/churn suffers because the value-to-price ratio doesn't feel justified at renewal time. Raise price until it becomes a real drag on close rates, then back off slightly — that's roughly the ceiling.

### 2.3 Two pricing approaches, blend them

**A. Existing-solution comparison.** Price relative to what the market already pays for comparable value, using a pricing structure the buyer already recognizes (e.g., per-seat, annual-upfront — matching an incumbent's own per-seat, annual-prepay convention because it's already the buyer's default mental model). Benefit: you inherit the market's existing rationale for the pricing structure instead of having to sell a new pricing *concept* on top of a new *product* concept — introduce one innovation at a time, not two.

Illustrative example: a recruiting-tool incumbent costs roughly $8,000/year/seat (about $5,000 for agency recruiters) for roughly 4,000 search results on a query like "Ruby engineer in Dallas" and a cap of 100 in-platform messages/month. A challenger delivering 2-5x the search recall plus direct emails plus unlimited outreach — arguably worth *more* than the incumbent — priced at $6,000/seat/year, 25% cheaper. Deliberately underpricing relative to delivered value makes the buying decision easy for an unproven upstart.

**B. ROI / value-based pricing.** Set price as a fraction of the quantifiable value delivered (from step 1.3's cost model). For example, a service that recovers missed placement fees for recruiting agencies might know that 1 in 500 candidate submissions yields a recoverable fee averaging $15,000. Charging $20,000/year for monitoring implies the client should recover roughly $200,000 — roughly a 10x return (individual customers might range 6x-15x). One caution: don't charge a literal percentage of realized value/output — it creates reporting/instrumentation headaches and gives the customer an incentive to under-report captured value. Charge a known, fixed price *derived from* a defensible ROI model instead.

**Blend them when you don't yet have a rock-solid ROI case**: price at a premium multiple of the nearest comparable (e.g., "our solution doubles qualified applicants in our own tests, so we charge 150% of the comparable job-posting product's price") rather than inventing pricing from nothing.

### 2.4 Value alignment & thresholding (land-and-expand entry points)
Where possible, align pricing to usage/value so customers can start small and grow into more spend as they derive more value (Dropbox/Box: pay more for more storage; Marketo/HubSpot: pay more for more contacts). This is especially important for trial/freemium motions — gate the paywall at the point where the user has clearly already gotten value (Yesware: free email tracking up to a volume threshold, then paywall; LinkedIn: free searches/profile views up to a cap, then paywall).

**Thresholding** = pick an entry price low enough to clear the buyer's natural approval friction, so they can self-serve into the purchase:
- Individual can just buy it themselves (Yesware).
- Team/manager can expense it on a corporate card without escalation (Box, Slack).
- Department can fund it from discretionary budget (a $6,000/year annual license, for example, tends to fit this bucket).

Elicitation question: "Is there a natural purchasing threshold — individual card, manager's discretionary budget, department budget — that your entry price should land under, to make the first purchase frictionless?" Not every product supports a small entry point (e.g., org-wide HR systems don't decompose to a single team), but blocking the "just buy one seat to try it" motion makes the sales cycle harder — flag that tradeoff explicitly if it applies.

### 2.5 Do NOT segment pricing this early
Resist the pull to copy mature companies' 3-tier pricing pages (Box/Slack/Salesforce-style "Good/Better/Best, one marked Most Popular"). Two reasons this fails for an early-stage company:
1. You should already be narrowly focused on a single ICP (see Early Prospecting) — you shouldn't be selling to different-enough customer profiles that would justify different tiers/segments in the first place.
2. You don't yet know which features/usage patterns actually matter to which customer segment — segmenting on a guess just adds complexity without informational basis.

Segmenting is a legitimate later-stage move, to extract more from price-insensitive, higher-end customers once you understand your market. Early on it's a distraction from the actual open question: "does this price work for my one ICP, at all."

### 2.6 Pricing is iterative, not a deliverable to perfect before shipping
Same posture as narrative: don't wait for pricing to be "done" or "perfect" before using it in live sales conversations. Set a defensible initial number using the frameworks above, start selling, and adjust upward as you gather more market-reaction data points.

## 3. Elicitation Checklist (quick-reference, ask in order)

1. Problem: "What's the pain, in one or two jargon-free sentences?"
2. Who: "What job title is functionally responsible for solving it?"
3. Cost: "What's the unit cost of this problem — hard cost or opportunity cost — and can you scale it by company size?"
4. Status quo: "How do they solve this today (nothing / manual process / agency / competing product), and specifically why does that break down?"
5. What changed: "What shifted in tech, cost, or behavior that makes this newly possible or newly urgent?"
6. Solution: "What's the closest thing prospects already understand that you can anchor your explanation to?"
7. Proof: "Using the same units as the cost answer, what's your quantitative edge? What qualitative edges can you back with a metric? What third-party proof exists?"
8. Pricing comparators: "What do prospects already pay for the closest existing solution, and in what structure (per-seat, per-contact, flat)?"
9. Pricing ROI: "Can you model the dollar value delivered per customer with reasonable confidence? If not yet, what's your best comparable-based proxy?"
10. Threshold: "What's the natural budget-approval ceiling (individual card / manager expense / department budget) you want your entry price to clear?"
11. Segmentation check: "Are you tempted to offer 3 tiers already? If yes — do you actually have more than one ICP right now, or is this premature?"
