# Closing & Pipeline Management

This reference gives the agent a framework for helping a founder build (1) a negotiation playbook,
(2) a close-winning checklist, (3) a win/loss capture process, and (4) pipeline hygiene. Apply it by
eliciting the founder's specifics (below) and filling in `assets/closing-playbook-template.md`.

## 1. Negotiation: what to optimize for, in order

Negotiation only happens once the prospect is already convinced there's value — it's not a pricing
objection, it's a question of "rack rate or 30% below." The founder is not being trained to be a master
negotiator; the goal is a simple framework for getting deals across the line while protecting the business.

**The core priority order, and why:**

1. **Cash up front is king.** Early-stage companies should always prioritize getting more money in the
   door immediately. Rationale: accounts-receivable risk. The customer could go out of business, get a
   new purchaser who holds payments hostage, etc. Get the cash into the bank account while you can. An
   early-stage startup's cost of capital is high, so don't finance your customers.
2. **Length of contract, second.** Longer terms reduce churn risk — they buy more time for the customer
   to reach success before a renewal decision comes up.
3. **Everything else — price per seat/unit, automatic renewal, opt-outs — comes after.** These are the
   levers you retreat on last, if you must give something up.

The ideal deal, from the seller's side: a long contract, the entire value paid up front, that auto-renews.

**How to negotiate the levers:**
- Retreat **one increment at a time**. Don't concede two or three steps when the prospect would have
  signed at one step down.
- When the prospect asks for a concession on one lever, use it as an opportunity to move a *different*
  lever in your favor. For example: prospect wants a per-seat discount → counter by offering
  better pricing if they buy more seats or extend the term (e.g., push a 6-month ask to 12 months —
  costs more in total but is cheaper per-period, and the prospect still feels like they got a deal).
- A quick 5% discount to close a deal — without touching payment terms — is often a steal for the
  founder, because it frees up time to move to the next deal. Conceding easily on a low-stakes lever and
  winning the deal is often the faster path to the next win.

**Basic negotiation tactics:**

| Prospect wants... | Founder response |
|---|---|
| Cheaper price per unit | Lower per-unit price only in exchange for more volume (more seats, more clicks/postings) or longer contract length. |
| Cheaper total (fixed budget, e.g. "only $10k") | Reduce seats/volume and raise the per-unit price, OR shorten the contract. For example: prospect wants 2 seats (normally $14k/yr) but has $10k — offer 6 months for $10k, or (better for the founder) offer a full year for $12k, which the prospect perceives as the better deal. |
| Shorter duration / shorter pilot | Charge **2–3x the normal per-period rate** for the shorter term (see rule below). |
| Split-up payments (quarterly, monthly, biannual) | Raise total price to compensate for AR risk and cost of capital — e.g., $10k/yr if paid up front becomes $12k/yr if paid quarterly. |

## 2. The shorter-duration markup rule

**Rule: if a prospect wants a shorter initial contract, price that shorter term at 2–3x the per-period
rate of the full-year price.**

For example: a six-month proposal priced at five thousand dollars, compared to seven thousand for the
whole year — the 6-month rate is disproportionately higher per period than the annual rate.

**How to explain this to a prospect who pushes back:** onboarding is a largely fixed cost, and it's very
hands-on (heavy customer-success labor). That onboarding cost has to be amortized across whatever
term the customer buys. Spread across twelve months, it's a small drag on unit economics; spread
across six or three months, it's a much bigger drag — hence the higher per-period price for shorter
terms. This is a legitimate cost-based rationale, not a punitive markup, and founders should feel
comfortable stating it plainly.

The same logic applies to split-payment requests: a customer paying quarterly puts the company at AR
risk and raises the effective cost of capital, so the total price should be higher than the up-front price
(e.g., $10k up front vs. $12k paid quarterly). If the customer has the capital, they'll usually take the
up-front discount once they see the math — and that saves the founder collections headaches later.

**Other negotiation notes worth carrying into the playbook:**
- Anticipate negotiation by starting proposals with inflated ("sticker") pricing, especially when selling
  to buyers experienced with enterprise software/procurement — they expect to negotiate down, and
  procurement departments will often try to wrangle a further discount on top of whatever was agreed
  with the direct decision-maker.
- Have an authority backstop ("let me check with my VP of Sales") to justify pushback on discounts, and
  feel free to explain plainly that the company needs revenue to deliver the service, pay engineers, and
  keep the lights on — this can humanize the negotiation.
- On competitor pricing claims: ask (politely) to see the competing proposal before matching or
  discounting, and only offer relief if the prospect commits to signing the same day the new pricing is
  delivered — otherwise you're just feeding a reverse auction between you and the competitor.
- Urgency levers: offer the rest of the month free if mid-month; note limited customer-success onboarding
  slots; note that current pricing/discount is only valid this month; note prices may rise in the future.

## 3. Close winning

Getting a "yes" is not the finish line — "run all the way until the money's in the bank," then make sure
the customer is actually up and running.

**Order forms and contracts:**
- Execute the contract as fast as possible once price is agreed — don't leave room for second thoughts.
- The order form is *not* the same as the proposal (marketing collateral). It exists purely to memorialize
  already-agreed terms and enable signature — keep it simple, friction-free.
- Don't attach legalese to the order form itself — bundling many pages of legalese in with the order
  form just raises questions and slows the deal. Host the MSA separately (corporate counsel template or
  a standard open-source startup sales-agreement template) and link to it.
- Always use e-signature software (HelloSign, Adobe eSign, etc.) — fire the order form over live, on a
  call or in the room, and get it signed on the spot. Store executed contracts in the CRM, tied to the
  opportunity.
- If procurement/legal wants to redline the contract: option A is to hold firm that your paper is the
  paper, cave only if there's real pushback; option B is to get corporate counsel to review redlines, but
  watch legal spend — it can eat a meaningful chunk of a small deal's value. Selling to unsophisticated
  buyers ("deer") without legal/procurement functions avoids this problem entirely.

**Getting paid:**
- Make payment frictionless — e-payment (PayPal, credit card) is worth the ~3% fee versus the cost of
  chasing collections or the risk of non-collection. Cash is king; a small fee is cheap insurance.
- Prefer being paid up front for annual contracts rather than setting up recurring billing infrastructure
  (Stripe/Recurly/Zuora) — that's a later-stage problem. For now, optimize for ASAP full payment and
  avoid AR headaches.

**Customer success handoff — capture at close, not later:**
- Treat "closing" → "succeeding" as one continuous motion. Get a kickoff call/training actually
  calendared before you move on.
- Capture, at close: name, title, and contact info for the decision-maker, executive sponsor(s), and
  end user(s) — especially when these are different people. You'll need this later to report success back
  to stakeholders.

## 4. Close losing and win/loss capture

Winning every deal is not a good sign — it usually means the pipeline is too thin or the founder is
cherry-picking only the best-qualified prospects instead of taking enough shots on goal. Typical win
rates for a new solution (or a newly forming market) are **10–20%**; **30%** is doing great. This is
normal, not something to be ashamed of.

**When to close something out:**
- Best case: the prospect gives a direct answer that she's not moving forward now — this is actually
  valuable because it lets the founder ask specifically why (competition? value not proven? no real
  need?) and calibrate whether it's resurrectable.
- More common: the prospect goes dark or cites no budget — usually "not now," not "no." The need
  likely still exists.
- Signal to watch for: broken "micro-contracts" — missed calls, difficulty scheduling a next meeting.
  Always keep a next meeting on the calendar so ambiguity surfaces quickly.
- When it's clearly stalling, send a direct, respectful "breakup" email rather than let it rot in the
  pipeline: acknowledge missed follow-ups, restate the ROI case, and ask directly whether they're still
  interested, whether timing is off, or whether it's genuinely not a fit. Don't burn the bridge — the
  contact may resurface at a new company, or the deal may reopen in a few months.

**What to record in the CRM when closing lost (the "closed lost metadata"), and why:**
- **The reason**, so the opportunity can be picked up later if resurrectable (as a *new* opportunity on
  the same account).
- **Competitive losses**: use a picklist field so the founder can see, in aggregate, who deals are being
  lost to.
- **Pricing/value-perception losses**: use a free-text notes field, and actually take the time to write
  detail — "a treasure map for your future self." In 2-3 months the specific context will be forgotten
  otherwise.
- This data also feeds back into product roadmap decisions and — for this skill's purposes — into
  refining the narrative and ICP: recurring loss reasons are signal about who to target and how to pitch.

**Coming back around:**
- Don't leave stale opportunities open, limping along with no next action — close them lost.
- But do set a resurrection date: ask the prospect directly when timing might be better, note it, and
  reengage then. If unclear, default to **60 days**.
- Reengagement should be a *new* opportunity, not a reopened old one, and should lead with what's
  new (new features shipped) to create fresh urgency.
- A prospect remains a viable target until they stop having the need (e.g., stop hiring, go out of
  business) or adopt a competitor. Discovery/demo investment already made is worth harvesting again.

## 5. Pipeline management

**Staging — the core discipline:**
- Every opportunity should be tagged with a specific, crisply-defined stage that means the same thing
  to everyone using the CRM (including future reps). Don't rely on generic CRM defaults (e.g.,
  Salesforce's stock "needs analysis" stage and associated close percentages) — define stages specific to
  your actual sales process.
- An illustrative stage list from a recruiting-tool startup (a concrete, adaptable example): **Demo Scheduled → Qualified →
  Seeking Approval → Sent Proposal → Negotiation → Verbal Agreement → Contract Sent → Closed
  Won / Closed Lost / Closed-Unqualified.**
  - *Qualified*: demo done, indeterminate next step ("thinking about it," "talking to my team") — this
    was the catch-all and, in hindsight, should have been broken down further.
  - *Seeking Approval*: pain validated, engaged stakeholder wants to proceed, but another
    stakeholder's approval is still being sought.
  - *Sent Proposal*: prospect specifically requested a proposal to consider pricing; next step is an
    up/down decision or negotiation.
  - *Negotiation*: all other stakeholder hurdles cleared; only price/terms remain.
  - *Verbal Agreement*: pricing agreed verbally, contract not yet sent — should be a short-lived stage.
  - *Contract Sent*: watch like a hawk — price agreed, contract out, waiting on signature.
  - *Closed-Unqualified*: a special stage for opportunities that should never have gotten a demo —
    track these to diagnose qualification failures upstream.
- Every stage needs an **explicit next step**, recorded in the CRM, with a **calendared date** for it.
  Pipelines go sideways when there's no next step, or a next step with no date attached.
- Use "time in current stage" reporting to catch stuck deals (e.g., a contract sitting unsigned for 9 days
  is a red flag worth chasing down).

**Cadencing:**
- Build a recurring, calendared cadence for pipeline review — don't rely on memory. A standing 2-hour
  weekly pipeline review block is a reasonable starting cadence.
- Calendar next-actions directly onto meetings whenever possible ("past you" forcing "future you" to
  follow up). When that's not possible, block dedicated pipeline-review time.
- Learn your personal "carrying load" — the number of concurrent opportunities you can support before
  close rates start dropping — and throttle new top-of-funnel volume once you hit it.

**Prioritization and cleaning — work "close to the money":**
- Review pipeline bottom-up: contracts out and verbal agreements first (most invested, most likely to
  close), then proposals, then earlier stages.
- Within a tier, prioritize by deal size — a $30k deal check-in is worth 3x the time of a $10k deal
  check-in for the same effort.
- Be ruthless about cleaning: opportunities with no future meeting and unanswered emails are "taking
  up room for a much more deserving opp." Send a breakup email and close them lost if there's no
  response.
- Useful hygiene reports: down-funnel opps (Seeking Approval and below) with no activity in 7 days;
  opps stuck in a stage 2+ weeks; "uncovered opps" with no future meeting on the calendar.

**Calendar/role notes (context, lighter-weight for this mode):** block time by activity type
(prospecting, outreach, follow-up, pipeline cleaning); mornings favor prospecting, midday/early
afternoon favor demos and calls, end of day favors wrap-up and follow-through; Mondays/Fridays have
higher demo cancellation rates. Once a founder is fielding enough qualified demos, hiring an SDR to
own top-of-funnel appointment-setting is the standard lever to unlock more down-funnel selling time
(cited cost-of-sales math: ~22–26% with an SDR+AE structure vs. ~30% for a full-cycle rep alone).

## 6. Elicitation questions for the agent to ask the founder

Use these to fill in `assets/closing-playbook-template.md`. Don't invent answers — ask.

1. What's your standard contract term and price today (per seat/unit, annual)? What would you charge
   for a shorter pilot or trial period, and does it currently reflect the 2–3x per-period markup rule?
2. When a prospect asks for a lower total price or a lower per-seat price, what do you currently offer in
   trade (fewer seats, shorter term, longer term for a discount)? Do you have a documented set of
   trade-offs, or does it vary deal to deal?
3. Do you require payment up front, or do you allow split payments? If split, do you currently charge a
   premium for that, and if not, what would a fair markup be given your cost of capital?
4. Who is your buyer, and do they typically have a procurement function? Do you currently pad your
   sticker price in anticipation of negotiation?
5. What does your order form / contract process look like today — do you have a simple order form
   separate from the full MSA, and are you using e-signature?
6. What do you currently do — if anything — to capture why a deal was lost? Do you have a picklist for
   competitive losses and a notes field for pricing/value losses, or is this undocumented right now?
7. What's your current close rate, and how many active opportunities are typically in your pipeline at
   once? (Use this to sanity-check whether the founder is qualifying too tightly or has too few deals in
   flight.)
8. What stages does your pipeline currently use, if any? Are they specific to your sales process, or are
   you using generic CRM defaults?
9. Do you have a habit of reengaging closed-lost opportunities after a set interval (e.g., 60 days), or do
   they tend to go untouched once marked lost?
10. What information do you currently capture at close time (decision-maker, exec sponsor, end-user
    contact info) to hand off to onboarding/customer success?
