# Objection Playbook — Framework

## What this mode does

Objections are proof the prospect is engaged, not silently drifting away or
giving a content-free "not a fit." This mode helps a founder build a written
playbook, one section per generic objection category, so responses are
rehearsed rather than improvised live.

Two framing points to carry into every category:

1. **Handle objections directly, not softened into mush.** Drawing on the
   idea of "respectful contentiousness" popularized in sales literature
   (notably *The Challenger Sale*): when an objection runs counter to the
   prospect's business reality, address it head-on with business-based
   arguments and proof.
2. **The best objection handling happens upstream, during discovery and
   pre-call planning** — not in the moment. Knowing the buying process and
   decision-maker(s) *before* an authority objection surfaces; gathering
   quantified need signals *before* being asked to re-justify need;
   understanding existing budget allocations *before* "no budget" comes up.
   If a category below keeps recurring for the founder, the fix is usually
   upstream discovery, not a better comeback line.

For vague brush-offs ("not sure it's a fit," "let me think about it"), a
useful elicitation line: **"What specifically is blocking us from
progressing right now?"** — forces content-free objections into concrete,
addressable ones.

## The seven generic objection categories

### 1. Lack of Decision-Making Authority
**What it is:** Prospect is sold but says someone else (boss, CFO) must
sign off.
**Resolution:** Validate it's the *real* blocker first — "Is the authority
to make this decision the only thing blocking us from getting you
started?" (surface other objections now, not in front of the real
decision-maker later). Then take the ball yourself, framed as "helpful" —
offer to run point on booking the decision-maker meeting, since advocating
for the deal isn't the champion's day job. Treat that meeting as a second,
foreshortened demo pitched to what a senior stakeholder cares about (ROI,
not features) — they're often just back-checking their deputy's judgment.
Script: *"I'd love to help [decision-maker] understand how this helps with
[problem]. I know you're busy — let me take point on setting an
appointment so we can both present. Is her calendar available to you?"*
**Upstream fix:** Identify the likely decision-maker (via LinkedIn, etc.)
during discovery/pre-call planning, when prospects are far less guarded
about that information than late in a closing conversation.
**Elicit:** "Who typically holds real purchase authority vs. who you first
talk to?" / "How do you research the likely decision-maker before a call?"
/ "What convinced a senior stakeholder fastest when you've had to loop one
in?"

### 2. Lack of Need
**What it is:** The most concerning objection — signals either weak
discovery, or the prospect doesn't grasp the value, or doubts the org will
capture it.
**Resolution:** Re-verify need using the specific signifiers gathered in
discovery, stated concretely: *"Based on our discussion... your company
has fifty outside sales reps, and struggles getting them to log activity
in the CRM. Is this not the case?"* A **specific, contradicting fact**
("only five are outside reps") is real signal discovery missed something —
take it seriously. A **squishy, non-specific reply** ("I just don't think
we need it") is code for a different real objection (budget, authority,
priority) hiding behind "no need" — invite honesty: *"It appears your
organization definitely has the need, but perhaps something else is
blocking us... you can be straight with me."* Goal: convert vague brush-off
into a specific, addressable objection.
**Upstream fix:** Only survivable if discovery captured objective external
signifiers of demand up front; if not, the founder shouldn't have been
pitching this prospect.
**Elicit:** "What 2-3 objective signals tell you a prospect genuinely has
the need?" / "When a qualified prospect claims 'no need' anyway, what's
usually really going on — budget, authority, or priority?"

### 3. Fear of Change / "We're Happy With How We Do It Now"
**What it is:** Often disguised "no need" — prospect agrees the need
exists but resists changing her approach, or has a vague, nonspecific fear
your solution "won't work out" for her.
**Resolution — two sub-cases:**
- *Status-quo bias:* Make the hidden cost of inaction visible with actual
  numbers, pulled from your "Proof of a Better Solution" material. For
  example, an inclusive-hiring-language tool might frame it as: continuing
  the current approach is wasting fifteen to forty thousand dollars a
  year — half a Recruiting Coordinator salary. Tie the number to what the decision-maker's own stakeholders
  (CFO, VP Eng) care about. Layer in qualitative FOMO: position the
  purchase as the emerging standard, cite competitors already adopting it.
- *True fear of change (the "belief" term in the persuasion formula):* she
  believes the need and the solution but doubts execution will go well for
  *her*. Address with "how we'll make this easy for you" plus real customer
  success stories: *"Here are all the resources we have in place to ensure
  you capture the value. We will make you successful."*
**Elicit:** "What's the quantified cost of a prospect's status quo — do
you have a $-per-week/year formula?" / "What's your strongest proof
implementation goes smoothly for new customers?" / "Which competitors can
you credibly cite as already adopting solutions like yours?"

### 4. Timing is Bad / Higher Priorities
**What it is:** A better variant of "fear of change" — need, fit, and
value are agreed, budget isn't the objection, but other things feel more
urgent right now.
**Resolution — three tactics:**
1. **Reduce perceived effort.** The real fear is "no time to roll this
   out" — emphasize ease of adoption and concrete support (e.g., "we have
   customer success specialists who run daily training webinars"), and
   even reframe a competing rollout as a *reason* to adopt now (process is
   already being changed).
2. **Reframe your solution as the higher priority** — discover what else
   is competing for their time/budget, then build the ROI case for yours
   over that alternative (watch for commitment bias if they're already
   deep into the other option).
3. **Neutralize rather than fight timing** — get the deal signed now,
   started later. Levers: "14 months for the price of 12," "pricing goes
   up next year," "buy now, start in a month." Especially effective near
   fiscal year-end.
**Elicit:** "What's the most common reason a qualified prospect says timing
is wrong for you specifically?" / "What makes adoption feel closer to
zero-effort for the buyer?" / "What pricing inducement could you offer for
sign-now-start-later?"

### 5. Price / Value
**What it is:** A good place to be — prospect wants the deal, now
haggling. Critical distinction: **discounting** (posturing for a number)
vs. **value justification** (doesn't grasp the ROI).
**Resolution:** Diagnose which conversation it is: *"I'm happy to talk
about the value... but could you help me understand what you think would
be a fair price?"* or *"Are we far apart? How far?"* ~10% below quote =
discounting (handle in negotiation). ~50% below quote = real value gap.
For a real gap, walk the ROI case using their own discovery numbers — for
example, a fee-recovery service can compute expected recovered revenue from
the prospect's own submission volume and fee size, then ask them directly
where the analysis seems to be falling down. If pushback reveals a **legitimate rationale**
(their real numbers differ, they'll only use part of the product, their
current alternative already captures some value) — that's signal your
pricing model doesn't fit this segment. Early-stage guidance: favor
getting the deal done over holding a hard line, but **document the
exception's rationale** (ideally in the contract) so it's revisited at
renewal and doesn't become informally "standard." If a prospect genuinely
won't get much value, pass — that's a qualification failure, not a price
objection.
**Elicit:** "How far below quote does a prospect typically land when it's
discount-posturing vs. a real value gap?" / "What ROI case can you walk
through using their own numbers?" / "What comparable price point can you
anchor against?" / "What 2-3 legitimate reasons might a prospect get less
value than your typical customer, and what exception fits each?"

### 6. Budgeting Challenges
**What it is:** Usually a failure of imagination about *where* the money
comes from — not real absence of capacity — unless it's a red herring for
a different objection, or the org has no process at all for buying tools
(a disqualifier, not an objection, that discovery/BANT-ANUM should have
caught).
**Resolution — concrete budget-sourcing moves:**
1. **Find adjacent existing budget** for the *problem*, even without a
   line item for your exact solution (e.g., a recruiting tool redirecting
   part of an agency-fee budget toward a sourcing tool instead).
2. **Time to an upcoming renewal** — reallocate part of a renewing
   contract, bridge the gap with free months.
3. **Cross-bucket transfer**, e.g., headcount into tooling (Zendesk:
   automation cuts needed new-hire support agents, converts to a dollar
   figure that funds the purchase) — usually needs finance (CFO/controller)
   looped in, which helps since they think in ROI terms.
4. **Discretionary/"experiment" budget** the boss or CFO holds aside — ask
   for it explicitly; expect to start small (single seat, short term) and
   work to make it "stick" (land-and-expand).
5. **One-off budget justification** — build a new bucket by helping the
   champion make a strategic case to finance ("should we reduce reliance on
   X generally?" not "should we buy one seat?") — more complex, can grow
   deal size meaningfully.
6. **Financing/payment restructuring** for cash-constrained orgs: monthly
   plan priced higher than annual (visible cost-of-waiting motivates them
   to find upfront cash), or a hybrid that lets them pre-pay once cash
   arrives (useful when your ROI itself generates near-term cash).
**Upstream fix:** Confirm in discovery/BANT-ANUM that the org has a track
record of buying tools for business pain at all.
**Elicit:** "What adjacent budget line is a prospect already spending
against this problem?" / "Do budget/contract cycles have renewal windows
to time proposals around?" / "Would monthly-vs-annual restructuring
realistically help close cash-constrained prospects, given your margins?"

### 7. Reluctance ("I Need a Trial/Reference")
**What it is:** Usually not literally a trial request — it's "I'm not
fully convinced, I want proof I can see myself," or an avoidance tactic
deferring a real "no." Caution: an unstructured, unguided trial usually
fails from non-usage, and that failure gets blamed on the product.
**Resolution — clarify what's actually wanted:**
1. Ask directly: *"What sort of further proof would you be looking for?"*
   Often the answer is proof collateral (case studies, ROI studies,
   references) already sitting in the deck appendix.
2. If they want their *team* to see it, that's really "another demo for a
   bigger audience" — offer it directly.
3. If they want hands-on exposure, offer a **guided walk-through /
   "ride-along"**: a scheduled session where the prospect drives but the
   founder guides throughout, ensuring real usage.
4. Only for large deals, consider a **structured, time-bounded pilot**
   with defined success criteria and required check-ins/training —
   specifically to remove the risk of non-execution.
5. For smaller deals, it's fine to hold the line: *"I can do an hour-long
   guided session where you have control, but I can't do an unguided
   trial."*
**Elicit:** "When a prospect says 'I need a trial,' what do they usually
actually want — collateral, a team demo, or hands-on time?" / "Do you have
proof materials ready to hand over immediately?" / "Does your product
deliver value unattended, or does it need guidance to avoid friction that
gets blamed on the product?"

## The generic objection-handling loop

Regardless of category, use this repeatable pattern:
1. **Catch** the objection — don't let it slide by.
2. **Turn it to the "question under the question"** — get to the real,
   specific concern.
3. **Respond** with quantitative and qualitative proof, backed by a
   specific slide/material where possible.
4. **Validate understanding**: "Does that answer your question?"
5. **Return to the close**, proactively surfacing remaining objections:
   "Do you have other questions, or are you satisfied this is a fit?"

Loop until no objections remain, or the prospect crosses the persuasion
threshold anyway.

## Building the founder's playbook

1. Walk through each of the 7 categories, asking the elicitation questions.
2. For each, capture: what the objection sounds like in the founder's own
   words/market, and the specific quantitative/qualitative response.
3. Flag which objections are symptoms of thin discovery (authority, need,
   budget qualification especially) — recommend upstream discovery/pre-call
   fixes, not just better comeback lines.
4. Fill `assets/objection-playbook-template.md` with the founder's answers.
5. Remind the founder: objections that recur "a couple of times" should
   become a dedicated appendix slide, and all objections/responses should
   live in a running document — future onboarding material for sales hires.
